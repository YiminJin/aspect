# Refining ahead of a growing frictional fault

## What the literature contributes

Heister, Wheeler and Wick solve the trial increment and ask whether the thresholded crack region reached an under-resolved element. If it did, they refine, interpolate the old accepted state, and solve the increment again. They reported one to three correction cycles during fast growth in their tests. Their phase convention has smaller values in the crack. This is a good reference algorithm for the proposed ASPECT model, where damage increases from zero. [CMAME 290 (2015), §4.2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2015.03.009)

Freddi and Mingazzi use a fraction of a local energetic damage-onset threshold to mark elements before visible damage appears. They also extend the refined region by a physical distance related to the width of the phase-field profile. This addresses a different problem from estimating the velocity of yesterday's crack tip. An initially stationary tip can have a growing region of material close to failure. Their procedure still permits repeated refinement. [Mesh refinement procedures for the phase field approach to brittle fracture, §§3–4](https://air.unipr.it/handle/11381/2912982)

Gupta and colleagues combine marking by effective crack-driving energy with damage-based marking on multiple mesh levels. They test brittle, cohesive and dynamic fracture, including AT1. Their results motivate retaining both a precursor ahead of the tip and a damage indicator behind it. None of these papers validates a particular threshold or lead distance for the present RSF model. [CMAME 399 (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2022.115347)

## A predictor for a longer fault

1. Keep the formed fault band resolved and place a fine disk around the accepted tip. For a first comparison, give it radius 4ℓ. At every accepted step, check the distance from the current tip to the first under-resolved cell. When that distance falls below 3ℓ, rebuild the region to recover 4ℓ before starting the next solve. The 2ℓ tip-direction neighborhood then has a reserve of 1–2ℓ. Ignore the physical box boundary when computing this distance. A warning while the previous interval remained resolved requires no time-step replay.
2. If the disk alone causes too many remeshes or misses, look for a set of intact cells ahead that are approaching the damage threshold. Use the shear-driving term already assembled by the material and phase residual. In a locally flat, intact AT1 neighborhood its mechanical part Y can be compared with Kf = Gc/[(8/3)ℓ], in the same energy-density units used by the code. A ratio approaching one indicates that shear driving is approaching the local cost of producing damage. As a first offline test, try warning ratios of 0.7 and 0.9. Near an existing diffuse band, use the signed one-sided phase residual for a positive local damage perturbation, including the gradient and DG face contributions, normalized consistently by its test mass. A projected KKT residual can vanish at a converged lower bound even as the energy margin changes. Save the unprojected one-sided margin where needed. Coarse resolution can underestimate stress concentration. keep the geometric buffer active.
3. Use the warning cells to extend a rounded fine region along plausible directions ahead of the tip, with enough transverse width for the full process zone to turn. A rapid increase of near-tip slip rate or an advancing slip front on the formed fault is a reason to refresh the prediction early. The slip rate measures displacement across a fault in m/s. Multiplying it by a time interval predicts slip distance, which cannot be used as the distance the fault will lengthen. If a reliable tip trajectory is available, its recent arc-length advance can inform how much additional computational mesh to prepare. It cannot bound the first advance after prolonged arrest. Use previously accepted data for the forecast. An early mechanics predictor is worth adding only if its cost is repaid by fewer full retries.
4. After each trial, use a guard to catch new connected damage developing on an under-resolved cell, including the agreed neighborhood required to resolve the growth. Use a documented phase/increment tolerance appropriate to the DG field and test its influence against the uniform reference. Treat rejected trials as disposable. Refine the enlarged region in one batch, transfer the accepted parent, and retry the same physical interval. Preserve age, slip, formed geometry, native material history, phase bounds and irreversibility. Initially keep already refined cells. Later coarsening needs its own transfer and energy verification. Do not let marking decide which sections may form.
5. Test predictors retrospectively first. Hide all future frames when predicting. Compare the user's reactive rule, a buffered tip disk alone, and a buffered disk plus the energy warning. On existing uniform histories, count anticipated and missed advances, estimated correction intervals and fine cells per interval. Compare against the fixed wedge for this specimen. Uniform data supply an optimistic test since the predictor will subsequently see coarser fields. In actual AMR runs measure replayed nonlinear solves, remesh and transfer time, total wall time, work balance, event timing, and tip extension and path against the uniform reference. If the geometric predictor covers bursts economically, the energy indicator can wait.

Implement the criterion through ASPECT's existing mesh refinement infrastructure. The growing solver’s explicit rejection of adaptive meshes, its constraint and face assembly, and checkpoint/transfer of cell and quadrature histories are independent engineering requirements. The present strip layout depends on the finest bulk cell anywhere in the box. Retain consistent physical station identities across remeshing so an unrelated marked cell cannot renumber the fault or reset RSF state. The forecast changes the mesh. It does not change the tip law, RSF law, or timestep admissibility test.
